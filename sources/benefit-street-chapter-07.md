# Benefit Street — Chapter 7

## Midnight, October 1st

Ryden had been in the carriage house since eleven. He had checked the transmitter three times, which was two more times than necessary, and had spent the remaining time sitting at the bench with the headphones on, listening to what was in the air. Ships. The Newport naval station's regular traffic. A faint European something that faded before he could place it.

At midnight exactly he moved to the transmitter key.

He had not planned anything ceremonial. There was nothing to plan — the procedure was the procedure, the same it would have been for any operator on any night. He checked the frequency. He checked the power. He put his hand on the key.

---STATION 1RJ — TRANSMISSION LOG---
DATE: 01 OCT 1919  TIME: 0000  FREQ: 200M  OP: J.R.
CONDITIONS: CLEAR, LIGHT QRN

1RJ: CQ CQ CQ DE 1RJ K

        3ZZ: 1RJ DE 3ZZ — UR SIG WEAK BUT READABLE — RST 339
             QTH HARTFORD CT — HW? DE 3ZZ K

1RJ: 3ZZ DE 1RJ — R R TNX — RST 449 — QTH PROVIDENCE RI
     NEW STN — 73 DE 1RJ K

        3ZZ: 1RJ DE 3ZZ — R — FB — 73 ES GL DE 3ZZ AR

---LOG NOTE: First transmission 1RJ. Hartford, 90 mi. Weak
but solid. J.R.---

He sat for a moment after the exchange ended. Hartford, Connecticut. Ninety miles. A weak signal, a brief exchange, the standard courtesies. Nothing remarkable.

He noted the time — twelve minutes past midnight — and took the headphones off and went to bed.

---

## After School, October 1st

They knew the date. They had known it for months.

The boys came in at three with the particular quality of containment that Ryden had learned meant something was being held back. Colin's version was visible — he came through the kitchen door slightly too casually, as if not coming through it with any specific purpose. Kevin's version was invisible, which meant only that it was Kevin's. Jack set his books down and turned and waited.

*It went out at midnight*, Ryden said. *Hartford came back. Ninety miles.*

Colin said: *That's all?*

*That's the first contact*, Ryden said. *Come to the workshop.*

---

Kevin sat at the key.

This had not been discussed. Ryden had simply stepped back from it when they came in, and Kevin had come forward and sat down with the naturalness of someone taking a place that had been waiting for him. Colin stood at the bench. Jack stood slightly behind and to the right of Kevin, which was where he stood when he wanted to see something clearly without interfering with it.

Kevin put his hand on the key.

He sent the CQ exactly as it should be sent — each element formed precisely, the spacing between letters exact, the spacing between words exact, the whole thing at a measured pace that conveyed neither hurry nor hesitation. It was, Ryden thought, the most correctly formed CQ he had heard since the Signal Corps, where correct form was enforced.

They waited.

---STATION 1RJ — TRANSMISSION LOG---
DATE: 01 OCT 1919  TIME: 1542  FREQ: 200M  OP: K.D.
CONDITIONS: CLEAR, MODERATE SIGNAL

1RJ: CQ CQ CQ DE 1RJ K

        1AW: 1RJ DE 1AW — RST 469 — QTH BOSTON MA —
             NEW STN? — HW? DE 1AW K

1RJ: 1AW DE 1RJ — R R — RST 579 — QTH PROVIDENCE RI —
     NEW STN YES — FB SIG — 73 DE 1RJ K

        1AW: 1RJ DE 1AW — R — WELCOME — 73 DE 1AW AR

---LOG NOTE: Good. — K.D.---

Colin looked at Kevin's log note. *That's all you're going to write?*

Kevin looked at the log. Considered. Added nothing.

*It's enough*, Jack said.

---

## October — The Log Grows

The after-school sessions ran from half past three to half past four, six days a week. Ryden sat at the bench during the sessions, not at the key — that was the boys' position now — monitoring on the second receiver, occasionally making a note. The contacts built slowly and then less slowly as other stations began to recognize 1RJ and come back unprompted when they heard the call sign.

---STATION 1RJ — TRANSMISSION LOG---
DATE: 09 OCT 1919  TIME: 1604  FREQ: 200M  OP: C.D.
CONDITIONS: FAIR, QRM FROM UNKNOWN STN

1RJ: CQ CQ CQ DE 1RJ K

        2XF: 1RJ DE 2XF — RST 449 — QTH NEW YORK NY —
             PSE QRS — HW? DE 2XF K

1RJ: 2XF DE 1RJ — R — SRI — RST 569 — QTH PROVIDENCE RI —
     220 MI — 73 DE 1RJ K

        2XF: 1RJ DE 2XF — R — FB — 73 AR

---LOG NOTE: New York. 220 mi. 2XF asked me to slow down.
I was not going fast. C.D.---

Ryden read Colin's log note and said nothing. The QRS — *please send slower* — was standard courtesy from a station receiving a weaker signal, not a comment on Colin's speed. He had been going fast. Ryden made a note of the distinction between the two interpretations and decided this was not the moment.

---STATION 1RJ — TRANSMISSION LOG---
DATE: 17 OCT 1919  TIME: 1551  FREQ: 200M  OP: J.M.
CONDITIONS: EXCELLENT, NO QRM OR QRN

1RJ: CQ CQ CQ DE 1RJ K

        1YK: 1RJ DE 1YK — RST 599 — QTH PORTLAND ME —
             UR SIG PERFECT TONIGHT — WX? DE 1YK K

1RJ: 1YK DE 1RJ — R — TNX — RST 589 — WX CLEAR COOL —
     GOOD PROPAGATION THIS EVE — HW UR WX? DE 1RJ K

        1YK: 1YK DE 1RJ — WX SAME — AGREE RE PROPAGATION —
             SOMETHING IN THE AIR TONIGHT — 73 DE 1YK AR

---LOG NOTE: Portland, 210 mi. RST 599 — best signal report
to date. Atmospheric conditions exceptional from 1530 onward,
improving. Note correlation with low pressure system moving
offshore — check against tomorrow's conditions. — J.M.---

Ryden read Jack's log note twice. The correlation he'd noted between atmospheric conditions and signal quality was correct and was the kind of observation that took most amateur operators years to begin making. He put the log down and went back to his bench and didn't say anything, which was the right response.

---

The contacts by the end of October: Hartford, Boston, Providence local, New York, Portland, a station in Albany, two in New Jersey, one in Philadelphia. The furthest was Philadelphia, 280 miles. The log had sixty pages of entries in three handwritings.

On the wall of the carriage house Jack had pinned a map of the northeastern United States with each contact marked — a small circle, the call sign, the date. The cluster around New England was dense. The furthest circles were sparse. He looked at it sometimes after the sessions, not saying what he was looking at.

Ryden knew what he was looking at.

---

On the morning of October fifteenth Jack came downstairs earlier than usual and found Ryden in the kitchen.

*The twins' birthday*, Jack said. *Today. They're nine.*

Ryden looked at him. He had not known the date. He should have asked — it occurred to him now that he had never asked, which was the kind of omission that revealed something about how he had been thinking about the situation in April, when he had not been thinking about it clearly.

*What do they like*, he said.

Jack considered. *Colin likes things that go fast*, he said. *Kevin likes — * He paused. *Kevin likes things that are exactly right.*

Ryden nodded. He put his coat on and went out.

He came back at noon with a flat package for Colin — a book on locomotive engineering, heavily illustrated, with detailed cross-sections of engines and speed records and the history of the great railway lines. And for Kevin, a set of geometric drawing instruments in a fitted wooden case: compass, dividers, ruling pen, set squares, protractor. Each instrument in its own velvet-lined slot, exactly placed.

He put them on the kitchen table without ceremony.

Colin opened his immediately, turning pages with the focused speed of someone confirming a suspicion. Kevin opened the case and looked at the instruments for a long moment without touching them. Then he lifted the compass out of its slot and held it carefully, examining the hinge, the points, the adjustment screw.

He looked at Ryden.

*Thank you*, he said.

Mrs. Callaghan, who happened to be in the kitchen, turned back to the range and said nothing, which was the right thing.

---

It was a Tuesday evening in mid-October. The twins had gone upstairs. Ryden was at his desk in the rear study and Jack appeared in the doorway with *Flatland* in his hand — he had been re-reading it, Ryden had noticed, the way he re-read things that mattered.

*The sphere*, Jack said. *When it passes through Flatland. The Flatlanders see a circle that appears and changes size and disappears.*

*Yes*, Ryden said.

*They can't see the sphere. They can only see where it intersects their plane.*

*That's right.*

Jack was quiet for a moment. He was looking at the middle distance in the way he did when something was arranging itself. *The signals*, he said. *The ones that travel further than they should. They go up and something sends them back.* He paused. *We're the Flatlanders. We're only seeing the cross-section.*

Ryden looked at him.

*The thing that sends them back*, Jack said. *We can't see it directly. We can only see what it does.*

Ryden was still for a moment. Then he said: *Come and sit down.*

Jack sat. Ryden turned from his desk and looked at the boy — ten years old, sitting straight in the chair, holding a book about two-dimensional beings with the particular quality of attention that Ryden had been watching since April.

*There's something I want to read to you*, Ryden said. *It will take a few evenings. It's difficult in places.* He paused. *Tell me when you lose the thread.*

*All right*, Jack said.

Ryden got up and went to the shelves.

## The Ouspensky Sessions

The book was a manuscript — handwritten pages, densely covered, in Ryden's translation from the Russian. He had been working on it for two years, returning to passages that resisted him, revising his renderings. It was not finished. He had never shown it to anyone.

He sat down, opened it to the chapter he had marked, and began to read.

*Positivism is very good when it seeks an answer to the question of how something operates under given conditions. But when it makes the attempt to get outside of its definite conditions, or presumes to affirm that nothing exists outside of these given conditions, then it is transcending its own proper sphere.*

He stopped. Jack was looking at him with the particular look — steady, running assessment — but with something additional in it that Ryden had not seen before. Not just assessment. Recognition.

*That's what the engineers are doing*, Jack said. *With the short waves. They know how signals behave under conditions they understand. So they say nothing exists outside those conditions.*

*Yes*, Ryden said. *Exactly that.*

He read on. He read for forty minutes. He read the passage about the limitations of sense perception, the passage about consciousness operating below its potential, the passage that began: *Divide in yourself the mechanical from the conscious, see how little there is of the conscious, how seldom it works, and how strong is the mechanical.*

He stopped there and closed the manuscript.

Jack was looking at the middle distance. Not the thinking-look this time — something quieter. Ryden waited.

*How do you get more of it*, Jack said. *The conscious.*

*That's the question*, Ryden said. *Ouspensky has some answers. They're not simple.* He paused. *And there are other people working on the same question from different directions.*

*Like who.*

*A man named Gurdjieff*, Ryden said. *I haven't met him. I expect to, eventually.*

Jack looked at him. The look running, arriving somewhere. *Do you think about it*, he said. *Whether you're mechanical or conscious. Right now, I mean.*

*All the time*, Ryden said. *That's the practice.*

*The I Ching*, Jack said.

Ryden looked at him. Jack looked back, steadily, not apologizing for knowing.

*Yes*, Ryden said. *Among other things.*

They sat for a moment in the quiet of the rear study, the lamp on, the manuscript between them.

*Same time tomorrow*, Ryden said.

*Yes sir*, Jack said.

## Late Night — The Other Frequencies

After the boys were in bed Ryden went back to the carriage house. Not every night — three or four times a week — after ten, when the atmospheric conditions were what he needed. He worked frequencies below 200 meters, where no one was officially operating and where, theoretically, nothing useful could happen.

Something useful kept happening.

On the fourteenth of October he reached a station that identified itself as G2XX, which was a British district 2 call sign — somewhere in the north of England. The exchange was brief and the signal uncertain, fading in and out of the static. But the contact was there. G2XX received 1RJ at RST 239 — weak, barely readable, real.

Providence to northern England. Three thousand miles. On a frequency the experts said was useless.

He logged it in a separate notebook that he kept in the desk drawer and not on the carriage house bench. The entry was precise: date, time, frequency, call sign, RST both ways, duration. He made no note of his own response to it, which was considerable.

A letter arrived nine days later. The return address was a village in Yorkshire he had never heard of. Inside, a brief note in careful English from an operator who confirmed the contact, gave his full call sign, and asked about Ryden's equipment. The letter was signed with a name Ryden didn't recognize and a set of initials after it that he did — a Theosophical society affiliation, European chapter.

He put the letter in the same drawer as the notebook.

The following week he reached a station in France.

## November — Mrs. Callaghan's View

Mrs. Callaghan came in on a Thursday morning in mid-November and stood for a moment in the kitchen doorway, as she occasionally did, taking stock before beginning.

The kitchen was clean — the boys had learned to clean up after themselves in the mornings before school, Jack enforcing it without appearing to enforce it. Breakfast dishes washed and stacked. The table cleared except for a notebook — Colin's, she recognized the handwriting on the cover — and a copy of *Flatland* with a strip of paper marking a page. She had read the first chapter of *Flatland* herself one afternoon in September, curious, and had found it less peculiar than expected and more unsettling.

She hung up her coat and went through the house in the way she had developed over the months — less a cleaning routine now than a reading of the household's state, the way a good nurse reads a patient. The library: the Jungle Book at a late chapter, two bookmarks, meaning Ryden and Jack had reached different points. Kevin's drawing folder on the side table, thicker than last week. Colin's flat cap on its peg. The geometric construction had been moved from the radio room to the library table, where it had been sitting for two weeks. Someone — she suspected Jack — had placed it next to the Klein bottle, which had also migrated downstairs.

She stood looking at the two objects for a moment.

The radio room door was ajar. She could hear the equipment's faint hiss from the landing. The log on the bench had grown to a second volume.

Upstairs, on the third floor: Kevin's drawings covered the entire wall above his bed now, spreading onto the adjacent wall. Mathematical and organic at once, repeating patterns, geometries she half-recognised from the diagram in the library. She had stopped trying to categorize them. Colin's cork board had acquired a map of the northeastern United States with small circles marked on it — she knew what those were — and a list of Morse abbreviations with Colin's own annotations beside some of them, questioning or confirming in a handwriting that was becoming more assured.

Jack's desk had three books open on it, face down, at different stages. His mathematics notebook had been replaced by a second one. On the shelf: *Walden*, the Fleming textbook, a small volume she didn't recognize with a Russian name on the spine.

She went back downstairs.

Ryden was in the kitchen when she arrived, which was not unusual anymore — he had adjusted his morning schedule, she had observed, so that he was in the house when she arrived rather than in the carriage house. She had not mentioned this. She made the coffee while he stood at the window with his own cup, looking at the garden.

*The boys are well*, she said. Not a question.

*Yes*, Ryden said.

She put the kettle on for her tea. *Jack's ahead of where he should be in the school*, she said. *His teacher sent another note.*

*I know.*

*You'll need to think about that.*

*I know*, Ryden said again. He was still looking at the garden. The aerial wire moved slightly in the November wind.

Mrs. Callaghan looked at him — the back of him, the coat he still wore indoors in cold weather, the slight forward lean that meant he was thinking rather than looking. Seven months, she thought. Seven months since she had walked in and found him with a foreign-looking child in his kitchen and a note on the table that said *staying for the time being.*

She thought about the three boys asleep upstairs on the night she had found Kevin on the landing with the bronze figure, which felt like years ago now. She thought about Colin saying *good morning* to her that first day with the air of someone conceding a point. She thought about Jack's smile.

*You're a good man*, she said again. As she had said in April.

Ryden turned from the window. He looked at her with the expression he had when he was going to say something that cost him something.

*Don't tell anyone*, he said.

Mrs. Callaghan permitted herself a small smile, which she directed at the kettle. *No fear of that*, she said.

## Jack — Late November

He woke at six on a Thursday in late November and lay in the dark for a while before getting up, which was something he had started doing without planning to — lying still in the morning, not asleep, just present with whatever was there.

What was there: the house around him, occupied. The sound of Ryden moving on the floor below, which meant the morning ritual was running — the yarrow stalks, the coffee. The smell of the coffee reaching the third floor. Colin's breathing from the next room, slow and even. Kevin's, lighter.

He had been thinking about the Ouspensky sessions. They had been running for three weeks now, an hour or so after the twins went up, in the rear study with the lamp on and the manuscript between them. Ryden read, stopped, answered questions when Jack had them, sometimes asked questions of his own. The questions Ryden asked were not tests — they were the questions of someone thinking alongside another person, which was different and which Jack had identified as different without having a word for the distinction.

*Divide in yourself the mechanical from the conscious.*

He lay in the dark and tried it — not for the first time. There was something there, a quality of attention that was available sometimes and not others, that he couldn't produce by effort but could perhaps notice when it arrived. The I Ching sessions, Ryden had said. He had started to understand what that was for.

Not fortune-telling. Attention practice. Beginning each day in a particular quality of wakefulness rather than sliding into habit.

He had been doing it, in some form, for as long as he could remember — this noticing, this watching what was actually there rather than what was expected. He had not known it had a name. He had not known it was unusual.

He lay with that for a moment. The house around him, the sound of Ryden below, the smell of coffee. Seven months since the lane. Since the carriage house door and the transmitter on the bench and a man who set down his tool and looked at him without immediately knowing what to make of what he saw.

He got up and went downstairs.

Ryden was at the kitchen window with his coffee, as he usually was. He turned when Jack came in.

Jack went to the counter and cut bread and put it on the range to warm, which was what he did in the mornings. He wasn't ready to speak yet and Ryden didn't require him to, which was one of the things about Ryden that Jack had identified early and continued to find remarkable.

Outside the November light was coming up over the hill, grey and flat, the aerial wire dark against it.

Jack looked at the aerial wire. He thought about the signals going up and hitting the invisible layer and coming back. He thought about what couldn't be seen from inside three dimensions. He thought about a man who had been working alone in a carriage house in April, and a boy in a lane watching the lights.

He didn't try to name what had happened between then and now. It wasn't that kind of thing yet, maybe. Or maybe it didn't need a name.

The bread was warm. He put it on the table.

*Mr. Ryden*, he said.

*Yes.*

*Tomorrow's session.* He paused. *Can we read the passage about consciousness again? The divide-in-yourself one.*

Ryden looked at him. The particular look running in reverse — Jack looking at Ryden, for once, steadily, without the assessment quality. Something else.

*Yes*, Ryden said. *We can do that.*

Jack sat down and they ate in the November morning, the house quiet above them, the aerial wire outside reaching up into the grey sky, the year turning toward whatever came next.
